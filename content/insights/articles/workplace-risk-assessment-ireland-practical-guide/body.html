A useful workplace risk assessment is a record of a decision, not merely a completed form. It identifies hazards in the work your people actually do, considers who might be harmed and how, records sensible controls, and makes it clear who will put any missing controls in place.

The short answer

Walk the work, identify its hazards, assess the likelihood and seriousness of harm, then eliminate or control the risks. Record the findings, talk to the people doing the work, and review the assessment when work or conditions change.

What the Irish rules require

The Health and Safety Authority (HSA) explains that employers must identify hazards, carry out risk assessments and prepare a written Safety Statement under the Safety, Health and Welfare at Work Act 2005. The results of the risk assessments should be written into the Safety Statement. Employees must be consulted and involved in the process. The employer remains responsible for the assessment and for implementing the improvements it identifies.

The law does not require one particular form or scoring matrix. The HSA provides examples and templates, but the assessment needs to fit the actual workplace. A copied assessment that overlooks local equipment, work practices or people cannot do the job merely because every box is filled in.

Start with the work, not the template

List the activities and locations you control. In a retail operation that may include deliveries, stock movement, cash handling, cleaning, waste handling, maintenance and lone or late work. At a hospitality site it may include kitchen equipment, hot surfaces, chemicals, contractors and guest-facing tasks. The examples are prompts to investigate, not a substitute for looking at your own operation.

Walk through the work with employees and supervisors. Observe normal work and the less frequent tasks that can be missed in an office review: opening a blocked drain, changing a gas cylinder, moving heavy stock or cleaning an awkward space. Check accident and near-miss records, maintenance issues, equipment instructions and relevant safety data sheets where chemicals are involved. Ask which steps people find difficult and whether the written procedure matches what happens during a busy shift.

Record each hazard clearly

“Manual handling” names a broad topic. “One person lifts 25 kg boxes from floor level into a cramped storeroom” describes an exposure you can act on. A clear entry identifies the task, the hazard, the people who could be affected and the harm that might result. Include cleaners, contractors, visitors and members of the public where their exposure is relevant.

Assess the risk in context

Consider how likely harm is, how serious it could be, and how many people may be exposed or how often. A simple low, medium or high rating may be enough for a straightforward task; specialist work may need a more detailed method or competent advice. The score is a way to prioritise action, not the outcome of the exercise.

For example, a wet entrance floor during a rush can affect staff and customers. The assessment should examine why it becomes wet, when it happens, what current measures are actually used and whether those measures work. A risk rating without that context tells a manager little about what to change.

The HSA advises employers to focus on significant work-generated hazards and choose a method appropriate to the organisation. If a risk needs action now, do not wait for the next scheduled review or for a document to be signed.

Choose controls that change the work

Start by asking whether the hazard can be removed. If it cannot, consider changing the task, equipment or layout before relying on instructions or protective equipment alone. For a heavy delivery, that may mean smaller loads, better access and suitable handling equipment, alongside training where it is needed. For a chemical task, it may mean considering a safer product and a different application method before deciding on gloves or ventilation.

Write the controls as actions a supervisor can recognise. “Take care” is difficult to verify. “Keep the delivery route clear; use the trolley provided; stop and report damaged wheels” gives the team something specific to do. Name an owner and a realistic completion date for any improvement that is not yet in place. Record temporary precautions while a permanent fix is being arranged.

Ask one practical question

If someone new to this shift read the assessment, would they know what to do differently? If the answer is no, rewrite the control in the language of the task.

Put the assessment to use

Bring the relevant findings into the Safety Statement and make them available to the people whose work they affect. Use short team briefings or task instructions to explain the controls. If training is required, make sure it addresses the actual risk and check that the employee can apply it. Managers should be able to see whether actions are complete and whether controls still work during normal operations.

For multi-site organisations, a common assessment can provide a starting structure. Each site still needs to check local layout, equipment, activities and differences in exposure. One central document should not conceal a site-specific problem. Keep a record of local changes and who approved them so that the current version is easy to find.

  • Can a worker identify the task and hazard from the assessment?
  • Does it say who could be harmed and how?
  • Are existing controls specific and actually in use?
  • Do outstanding actions have an owner and due date?
  • Has the team been told about the precautions that affect them?
  • Can a manager verify the changes in the real workplace?

Review when reality changes

Review the assessment when equipment, substances, layout, staffing or the way a job is done changes; when an incident exposes a gap; or when monitoring shows a control is ineffective. The Safety Statement should also be reviewed periodically. The HSA's guidance says it should be revised at least annually and whenever significant changes or new assessments affect safety and health.

Keep enough version information to show what changed, why, who reviewed it and when staff were informed. This prevents an old assessment from quietly becoming the working instruction. If a new control is promised, follow the action through to completion and check that it reduces the risk.
