A manual handling risk assessment should describe the real handling operation, the people exposed and the changes needed to reduce risk. Start by asking whether hazardous handling can be avoided. If it cannot, assess the task, load, working environment and individual capability, together with any handling aids, work organisation, pace, frequency and duration.

The short answer

Record what is moved, how, where, how often and by whom; observe the task as it is actually done; identify high-risk postures, forces, distances and conditions; choose the right HSE assessment tool where one is needed; specify controls, owners and due dates; and verify that the revised method reduces the risk. A generic “manual handling training completed” entry is not a task assessment.

When is a manual handling risk assessment needed?

Not every movement of a load needs a long standalone form. HSE provides simple filters to help employers screen out straightforward low-risk tasks. When handling may create a risk of injury and cannot reasonably be avoided, the employer needs a suitable and sufficient assessment of that operation.

Prioritise tasks that involve one or more of the following:

  • heavy, bulky, unstable, hot, sharp or difficult-to-grip loads;
  • lifting from the floor, above shoulder height or at long reach;
  • twisting, stooping, reaching sideways or holding a load away from the body;
  • frequent or prolonged handling, fast work rates or inadequate recovery time;
  • long carrying distances, steps, slopes, uneven floors or restricted space;
  • pushing or pulling equipment with high starting or stopping forces;
  • team handling where coordination, visibility or load sharing may vary;
  • people reporting discomfort, fatigue or difficulty with the task;
  • previous injuries, near misses or damaged stock and equipment; or
  • non-routine work that has not been designed or rehearsed.

The assessment should be proportionate. A low-risk, stable box moved occasionally between waist-height surfaces may need only a simple documented method. Repeated lifting from a pallet, moving an awkward assembly or manoeuvring a loaded cage on a slope needs closer examination.

Use the avoid, assess and reduce hierarchy

Regulation 4 of the Manual Handling Operations Regulations 1992 sets a hierarchy for Great Britain. Employers must, so far as reasonably practicable:

  1. Avoid hazardous manual handling operations.
  2. Assess hazardous operations that cannot be avoided.
  3. Reduce the risk of injury to the lowest level reasonably practicable.

This order matters. Do not begin with lifting technique if the load can be delivered closer to the point of use, moved mechanically, split into smaller units or removed from the process. The best assessment may conclude that the handling operation should not continue in its current form.

Where handling remains necessary, the assessment should produce a safer method, not merely a risk score. Record which risks will be eliminated, which will be reduced, what residual risk remains and who may perform the task.

What should the assessment record?

HSE advises employers to consider the task, load, working environment and individual capability. Its brief guide also points to handling aids, work organisation and the pace, frequency and duration of the work. A practical record should cover each of these areas.

The task

  • the start and finish points and the route between them;
  • lifting, lowering, carrying, pushing, pulling, holding or team handling involved;
  • load height, reach, carrying distance and vertical movement;
  • twisting, stooping, reaching, sudden movement or restricted posture;
  • frequency, duration, work rate, recovery time and peak periods;
  • whether precision, visibility or coordination is required; and
  • normal variation, interruptions and credible worst-case conditions.

The load

  • actual weight and whether it varies;
  • dimensions, shape, stability and centre of gravity;
  • grip, handles, packaging and the risk of contents shifting;
  • sharp edges, temperature, contamination or fragility;
  • whether weight and the heaviest side are clearly identified; and
  • whether the load can be reduced, divided, containerised or redesigned.

The working environment

  • available space, headroom and clearance for feet and hands;
  • floor condition, gradients, steps, doorways and changes of level;
  • lighting, temperature, ventilation, noise and visibility;
  • traffic, customers, other workers and simultaneous tasks;
  • storage heights and workstation layout; and
  • whether equipment can be positioned and used safely.

Individual capability

  • the range of people expected to perform the task;
  • strength, stature, experience and familiarity relevant to the work;
  • new, young, temporary or agency workers who may lack task knowledge;
  • new or expectant mothers, workers with disabilities and people returning after injury;
  • language, communication or instruction needs;
  • lone workers and contractors; and
  • reports of discomfort, fatigue, reluctance or difficulty.

Do not record sensitive medical details unnecessarily. The assessment needs to identify capability requirements, adjustments and restrictions while handling personal information appropriately.

Equipment and work organisation

  • the handling aid or mechanical equipment available;
  • its condition, capacity, compatibility and inspection status;
  • whether it is accessible at the point and time of need;
  • staffing levels, supervision and communication for team handling;
  • targets, deadlines or incentives that encourage rushing; and
  • maintenance, charging, storage and defect-reporting arrangements.

Observe the real work and involve the people doing it

A desk-based description can miss the very conditions that create the risk. Observe the task during normal work, including a busy period where that changes pace, stock position or available space. If the operation varies, assess the credible worst case instead of the neatest demonstration.

HSE advises consulting workers and their representatives because they know which activities are difficult and may have practical solutions. Ask:

  • Which part of the operation feels hardest?
  • When does the planned method stop being practical?
  • Which loads vary from the specification?
  • Is the handling aid available and usable when needed?
  • What happens when staffing is reduced or the route is obstructed?
  • Has anyone felt pain, lost control of the load or had a near miss?

Photographs, measurements or a short video may help a competent assessor understand posture and movement, but only where recording is permitted and privacy is controlled. Evidence should support observation, not replace speaking to the people who do the job.

Choose the right HSE assessment tool

The tool should match the operation. A familiar acronym or a single score is not automatically a suitable and sufficient assessment.

  • Simple risk filters: use these to identify straightforward low-risk lifting, carrying or pushing and pulling tasks and decide whether a detailed assessment is needed.
  • MAC: the Manual Handling Assessment Charts support assessment of lifting, carrying and team handling.
  • V-MAC: the Variable Manual Handling Assessment Chart can help with complex operations where load weights vary.
  • RAPP: the Risk Assessment of Pushing and Pulling tool is designed for pushing and pulling operations.
  • ART: the Assessment of Repetitive Tasks tool is relevant to repetitive upper-limb work.
  • Detailed HSE checklists: these can support more complex lifting, carrying, pushing or pulling assessments.

HSE notes that the MAC tool may not be a complete risk assessment on its own. It does not cover every possible risk, so the assessor still needs to consider individual capability, the environment, task-specific hazards and the effectiveness of controls.

Use scores to identify and prioritise risk factors, not to declare a task safe. HSE's MAC guidance says total scores do not correspond to specific action levels.

Select controls that change the risk

Controls should follow from the factors identified. Effective options may include:

  • eliminating the move or changing where materials are delivered and stored;
  • using a hoist, lift table, powered truck, trolley or other suitable aid;
  • reducing container size or load weight;
  • adding stable handles or improving packaging and grip;
  • raising or lowering work to avoid floor-level and above-shoulder handling;
  • shortening carrying distances and removing steps or obstructions;
  • rearranging the workstation to reduce reaching and twisting;
  • improving floors, lighting, space or temperature;
  • reducing frequency, pace or duration and providing recovery time;
  • defining when team handling is permitted and how it is coordinated; and
  • restricting the task until equipment, staffing or environmental conditions are suitable.

Assign each action to a named owner with a due date. Record the immediate control if a permanent engineering or layout change will take time. “Be careful” and “use correct technique” are not adequate substitutes for removing avoidable risk.

After making changes, observe the task again. A new trolley may reduce carrying but introduce pushing force, trapping, visibility or slope risks. Verification should confirm that the revised system works in practice and has not moved the hazard elsewhere.

Training and good technique support controls—they do not replace them

Workers need information and training suited to the task, equipment and controls. They should understand the load, route, handling aid, restrictions, stop-work conditions and how to report discomfort or defects.

Generic lifting instruction cannot compensate for a load that is too heavy, an obstructed route, an unavailable handling aid or unrealistic staffing. Apply the hierarchy first, then train people in the safer system that remains.

Where team handling is used, define who leads, how the team communicates, how the route is cleared and what happens if one person loses grip. Adding people without planning can create uneven load sharing and coordination hazards.

When should the assessment be reviewed?

Regulation 4 requires review where there is reason to suspect the assessment is no longer valid or where the operation changes significantly. Practical review triggers include:

  • an injury, discomfort report, near miss or loss of control;
  • a repeat event or warning from a similar task;
  • a new load, package, supplier or weight range;
  • changed equipment, layout, route, storage height or work rate;
  • a handling aid being unavailable, damaged or routinely bypassed;
  • new people or a change in who performs the task;
  • evidence that the written method does not match normal work; or
  • a planned review date based on risk and experience.

Keep the original finding and show what changed. Replacing a failed assessment with a clean version can hide whether corrective action addressed the actual cause.

Recent HSE reminder

On 7 October 2026, HSE reported a prosecution after a worker suffered a life-changing hand injury while repositioning a 133kg assembly. HSE found no task risk assessment or safe system of work, and a near-identical incident had occurred days earlier. The case enforces existing duties; it does not create a new rule.

How should multi-site organisations manage assessments?

A central template can standardise the factors, evidence, action priorities and review triggers. It should not assume that the same task has the same risk at every location. Door widths, slopes, storage heights, staffing, equipment and delivery arrangements can change the result.

Use a controlled master assessment only where the task and conditions are genuinely equivalent. Require each site to confirm local applicability and record deviations. Link the assessment to:

  • the task and work instruction;
  • the equipment or handling aid used;
  • people trained or authorised;
  • reported discomfort, incidents and near misses;
  • corrective actions and verification evidence; and
  • the current review status.

Trend recurring problems by task, load, site, supplier and equipment type. If several locations struggle with the same delivery container or storage arrangement, solve the design problem centrally instead of repeating local retraining.

What are the legal duties and what is guidance?

Great Britain: Regulation 4 of the Manual Handling Operations Regulations 1992 requires employers to avoid hazardous manual handling so far as reasonably practicable, assess operations that cannot be avoided, and reduce the risk of injury to the lowest level reasonably practicable. It also requires appropriate information about load weight and the heaviest side where reasonably practicable.

HSE's manual handling pages, brief guide, risk filters and assessment tools are guidance that help employers apply those existing duties. They are not new regulations, and completing an HSE tool does not by itself prove that every relevant risk has been controlled.

Northern Ireland: separate Manual Handling Operations Regulations (Northern Ireland) 1992 apply and are enforced by HSENI. Organisations operating across the UK should not label a Great Britain assessment as automatically covering Northern Ireland without checking the applicable requirements and guidance.
