A machinery guarding inspection checklist should do more than confirm that a guard is present. It should identify the machine and danger zones, test the guards and protective devices that matter, record any defect or route into moving parts, and show the decision made before the equipment returns to use.

The short answer

Record which machine was inspected, where it is, who inspected it and when; what guards, interlocks and safety devices were checked; the method and result; any defect or unauthorised change; whether the machine is safe to use; and the action, owner and verification needed before a failed machine is released.

What should the inspection record?

The record needs enough detail for another competent person to understand what was inspected, what was found and why the machine was allowed to run or kept out of use. A generic tick beside “guarding OK” cannot show which guard was checked or whether an opening still permits access to a danger zone.

  • Machine type, model, unique asset number and exact location
  • Date and time, inspector's name and their role or competence
  • Reason for the inspection: pre-use check, planned formal inspection, new installation, relocation, modification, damage, incident or prolonged inactivity
  • Guards, interlocks, protective devices and danger zones included in the inspection
  • Inspection method, including any functional test and the condition in which it was carried out
  • Defects, damage, gaps, missing fasteners, bypasses or signs of interference found
  • Evidence such as photographs, measurements or test results where useful
  • Immediate decision: safe to use, restricted use under an authorised control, or taken out of service
  • Corrective action, named owner, priority and due date
  • Repair evidence, verification by a competent person and return-to-use approval
  • Next inspection date or the rule that determines it

Ireland's Health and Safety Authority (HSA) gives a similar practical list for work-equipment records: type and model, identification, location, inspection date, inspector, extent of the inspection or test, faults, required remedial measures and the action taken. Under Ireland's General Application Regulations, relevant inspection results must be recorded and kept available for five years. Great Britain's Health and Safety Executive (HSE) says PUWER inspection records should be kept at least until the next inspection, although other record-keeping duties or company rules may require longer.

What should a guarding inspection check?

Start from the machine-specific risk assessment, manufacturer instructions and the way the equipment is actually used, cleaned, adjusted and unblocked. The checklist should direct the inspector to the dangerous parts and credible routes to them, not simply reproduce the same questions for every machine.

  • Fixed guards are secure, correctly positioned and free from damage, distortion, corrosion or missing fixings.
  • Openings do not allow a hand, arm, foot, tool or other body part to reach moving parts from above, below, around or through the guard.
  • Guards cannot be easily removed or defeated; any removal that should require a tool still does.
  • Interlocked guards stop the hazardous movement as designed and do not allow an unsafe restart when the guard is closed.
  • Light curtains, pressure-sensitive devices, trip wires and other protective devices operate across the intended zone and have not been bypassed.
  • Emergency stops and normal stop controls work, while recognising that an emergency stop is not a substitute for effective guarding.
  • Nip points, crush points, rotating parts, draw-in hazards and ejection risks remain controlled during normal operation and foreseeable tasks.
  • Recent modifications, temporary repairs, changed tools, new products or altered cleaning methods have not created a new access route or defeated a safeguard.
  • Instructions, warning labels and isolation points are legible and consistent with the current machine.

Do not test an interlock or reach distance in a way that exposes the inspector. Plan the inspection, use the manufacturer's safe test method and isolate energy where the check requires access beyond a guard. A visual pre-use check and a formal inspection are different activities; one does not automatically replace the other.

Avoid the tick-box trap

HSE warns that overly detailed checks carried out too often can become superficial. Tailor the checklist to the safety-related parts and known failure modes of the machine, then make each answer evidence-based.

How often should machine guards be inspected?

There is no universal weekly or monthly interval for every machine guard. Set the frequency through risk assessment, taking account of the manufacturer's instructions, severity of harm, intensity of use, environment, how quickly fasteners or interlocks can deteriorate, previous defects and experience with the equipment.

Operators may need a short check before each shift or use, while a competent person completes a more detailed inspection at planned intervals. Additional inspections may be needed after installation or reassembly, relocation, modification, a collision or other damage, an incident, exceptional circumstances or prolonged inactivity. Increase the frequency when defects are appearing between planned inspections; reduce it only when the risk assessment and inspection history support that decision.

Who should carry out the inspection?

The inspector must be competent for the inspection they are assigned. That means having enough knowledge and experience to identify the relevant safety-critical parts, recognise deterioration and unsafe modification, understand the limits of the inspection, and know what action to take when something is wrong.

A trained operator may be competent to carry out a defined pre-use check. A formal inspection of interlocked guarding, stopping performance or a complex safety-control system may require an engineer, specialist contractor or another person with deeper technical competence. Job title alone is not evidence of competence. Record the scope the person is authorised to inspect and when specialist help is required.

What should happen when a machine fails?

If the defect could expose someone to dangerous movement, do not leave the decision in a notes field for later. Stop the machine, isolate its energy where necessary, prevent use, identify it clearly and escalate the issue under the site's defect procedure. Record the immediate control even if the permanent repair will follow later.

Assign the repair to a competent person and preserve the original failed result. When the work is complete, record what was changed and verify that the guard, interlock and associated controls work before authorising return to service. Closing an action because a part was ordered is not the same as confirming that the machine is safe.

A serious defect on one machine should also prompt a proportionate check of similar equipment, especially where the same design, modification, maintenance contractor or operating practice is used at other sites.

Separate inspection from maintenance and isolation

An inspection identifies condition; maintenance restores or preserves it. Maintenance and blockage clearing can create greater risk because people may need to approach dangerous parts while normal safeguards are removed. Before that work starts, plan the task, stop the machine, isolate every relevant energy source and control stored energy. Electrical, hydraulic, pneumatic, gravity, pressure and thermal energy may all matter.

Use an effective lock-off or other secured isolation where unexpected restart could cause harm. Control who can remove the isolation, and check that guards and protective devices are correctly restored before restart. A functioning emergency stop does not make work inside a danger zone safe.

How should multi-site organisations manage the record?

A central template can standardise asset identifiers, defect ratings, required evidence and escalation rules. The inspection itself still needs to reflect the machine, guard design, environment and tasks at the local site. Keep a clear connection between the equipment record, its current risk assessment, maintenance history and outstanding corrective actions.

Supervisors should be able to see which machines are out of service, which inspections are overdue and which repairs await verification. Trend repeated failures by machine model, guard type, contractor and location. That turns inspection data into a control-improvement process rather than a collection of completed forms.

What do the Ireland and UK rules say?

Ireland: Regulation 30 of the Safety, Health and Welfare at Work (General Application) Regulations 2007 requires competent-person inspections in specified circumstances, including where installation conditions affect safety and where deterioration could create danger. It also requires periodic and special inspections where relevant. Regulation 33 requires guards or protective devices where contact with moving parts could cause accidents, and Regulation 34 addresses isolation from energy sources. The HSA guide is guidance and explains how the regulations may be applied; it is not itself legislation.

Great Britain: the Provision and Use of Work Equipment Regulations 1998 (PUWER) require suitable inspection where safety depends on installation or where deterioration may cause dangerous situations. Regulation 11 requires effective measures to prevent access to dangerous parts or stop their movement before a danger zone is reached. HSE guidance helps employers apply those existing duties. Northern Ireland has separate but similar PUWER regulations, enforced by HSENI, so organisations operating there should check the applicable local requirements.

On 29 September 2026, HSE reported a Great Britain prosecution following a fatal conveyor incident in which an opening in fixed guarding allowed access to dangerous parts. The company pleaded guilty to breaching PUWER Regulation 11(1). The case is enforcement of an existing duty, not a new rule, but it is a timely reminder that the presence of a guard is not enough if someone can still reach the hazard.
